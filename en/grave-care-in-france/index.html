Looking after a family grave in France from abroad

In France, a grave belongs to the family, and so does its upkeep. If nobody in the family lives nearby any more, here is what you need to know, and what you can arrange from wherever you are.

Updated 14 November 2026

In short

  • The family, not the town, is responsible for looking after a grave.
  • Most plots are leased for 15, 30 or 50 years. A lease can be renewed when it ends, and for two years after.
  • A plot over 30 years old, with no burial in the last 10 years and visibly neglected, can be taken back by the town after a procedure lasting at least a year.
  • The town hall keeps the cemetery register: it can tell you where a grave is and when its lease ends.

Who is responsible for a grave in France?

A French burial plot is a concession: the town lends the ground, and the family builds a monument that it owns. Upkeep falls to the person who holds the plot and, after their death, to all of their heirs together. The town only looks after the paths, walls and shared areas.

In practice, the relative who lives closest does the work. When the last of them moves away or dies, the grave is left on its own.

Plot leases, and what happens when they end

Most plots are leased for 15, 30 or 50 years, depending on the town; some older ones are perpetual. When a lease ends, the family can renew it, and still has two years after the end date to do so. After that, the town can take the plot back, however well the grave is kept.

If you do not know when the lease ends, ask the town hall (mairie) of the town where the cemetery is. It keeps the register of plots.

When a grave is considered abandoned

A town can also take back a plot that looks abandoned, even a perpetual one. French law sets three conditions, and all three must be met:

  • the plot is more than 30 years old;
  • nobody has been buried there for at least 10 years;
  • the grave is visibly neglected: a crumbling monument, overgrown with weeds.

The mayor records the state of the grave, informs the family when it is known, and posts a notice at the town hall and the cemetery, often with a small sign on the grave itself. The family then has one year to put the grave back in order. If nothing has changed after a second inspection, the town council can decide to take the plot back, and the remains are moved to the town’s ossuary.

If the grave is put back in order during that year, the procedure stops. Regular, visible care is the best protection.

Finding a grave from abroad

Write to the town hall with the full name of the person buried and the year of death if you have it. Ask for the name of the cemetery and the location of the grave: section (carré), row (allée) and number. A short letter or email in French gets the fastest answer.

If you ask us for a visit and do not know where the grave is, tell us the name on it and what you remember. When needed, we check the cemetery register with the town hall.

A few French customs

  • All Saints’ Day, 1 November, is when families visit and flower their graves. Cemeteries are at their busiest in the last two weeks of October.
  • Chrysanthemums are the flower of that day. In France they are for graves, not for dinner hosts.
  • Palm Sunday is a second grave-flowering day in northern France.
  • Stone is cleaned with clear water and a soft brush. Bleach and pressure washers damage it.

War graves are a separate case

Graves of Commonwealth soldiers are looked after by the Commonwealth War Graves Commission, and American military cemeteries by the American Battle Monuments Commission. A family grave in a town cemetery is not covered by either: it stays the family’s responsibility.
